The Great
Emu War

In 1932, the Australian Army sent three soldiers and two machine guns to fight 20,000 emus, and the emus won. The operation in Western Australia's Wheatbelt ran from 2 November to 10 December, killed roughly 986 birds by the army's own count, and barely dented the population. Farmers ended up relying on bounties and fences instead.

I. Why It Happened

1. Veterans on marginal land
1919–1932

The farmers were WWI veterans the government had settled on poor land. The Great Depression made it worse: they were told to grow more wheat, the promised subsidies never arrived, and wheat prices kept falling.

2. Veterans asked for machine guns
12 Oct 1932

The ex-soldiers knew what machine guns could do, so they asked Defence Minister Sir George Pearce for some. Perth's Daily News called it a "war" on 12 October 1932, the earliest recorded use of the term.1"The Emu War", Daily News, Perth, 12 October 1932

  • Pearce agreed, provided soldiers fired the guns and the farmers paid for food, lodging and ammunition.
  • A Fox Movietone cameraman came along, which suggests the government wanted to be seen helping.

II. How It Went

1. First attempt
2–8 Nov

The emus split into small groups and ran, so the guns had little to hit. At one dam ambush, more than 1,000 birds came within range, and then the gun jammed after only 12 kills.

  • 2,500 rounds were fired in six days, and estimates of birds killed range from 50 to 500.
  • Pearce withdrew the troops on 8 November after bad press.

The Emu command had evidently ordered guerrilla tactics, and its unwieldy army soon split up into innumerable small units that made use of the military equipment uneconomic.

Dominic Serventy, ornithologist
